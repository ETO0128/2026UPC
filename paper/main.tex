\renewcommand{\abstractname}{Summary}
\begin{abstract}
We model first-stage recovery with coupled downrange, vertical, and pitch dynamics, using a reference liquid-oxygen and methane propulsion design. A nominal one-ignition trajectory satisfies the selected touchdown criteria with a separation propellant inventory of approximately 26.23 t. Fixed-schedule perturbation cases motivate further guidance analysis. Planning separately for known initial-state and steady-wind variations gives eleven feasible cases at a common 30 t inventory and fixed ship position. The remaining assessment includes uncertain measurements, disturbances arising during flight, actuator response, and structural and thermal limits.

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background}
Reusable first-stage boosters require controlled descent and a safe vertical landing before refurbishment and reuse.
\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}
At separation, the booster has an altitude of 70 km and a speed of 6000 km/h. Its dry mass is 130000 kg, its height is 65 m, and its diameter is 7.0 m. It uses liquid oxygen and methane. The recovery ship may be placed at a convenient location. The task is to analyze a safe descent and controlled vertical landing, and estimate the fuel required at separation~\cite{problem}.
The problem specifies the initial speed magnitude. The direction affects the time aloft, downrange distance, and state at landing-burn ignition, so it is an explicit scenario input. We use \emph{propellant} for the combined mass of liquid oxygen and methane and \emph{fuel} for the methane component.
\section{Assumptions}
The booster is initially approximated as a rigid circular cylinder. This external geometry does not establish a uniform internal mass distribution. Atmospheric drag is retained using air-relative velocity and altitude-dependent density. The baseline translational model assumes planar motion and zero wind; steady uniform wind is a subsequent scenario, rather than a justification for neglecting aerodynamic torque.

Aerodynamic moments depend on the offset between the center of pressure and center of mass. A prescribed-axis translational model therefore cannot establish attitude stability or vertical touchdown. Rotational dynamics and control authority must be assessed separately.

Earth rotation is provisionally omitted from the baseline and requires a quantitative sensitivity check. Its omission is not justified by freedom in ship placement: Coriolis acceleration can alter velocity and consequently aerodynamic loading. The initial flight-path angle and all unspecified engine and aerodynamic parameters remain explicit scenario inputs.

The deck is initially stationary and horizontal. Position denotes the booster center of mass; contact geometry must account for vehicle length and, when specified, landing gear and deck height.

\section{Further Discussion}
\subsection{Attitude Stability and Vertical Touchdown}
The local pitch controller brings the body upright under the tested conditions, but the case with an initial pitch-rate error illustrates that attitude recovery can occur alongside a position miss. A full recovery trajectory must satisfy the position, velocity, tilt, and pitch-rate limits at the same contact event. Control authority also depends on gimbal saturation and whether the engine is on.
\subsection{Structural Loads and Aerothermal Constraints}
Section~\ref{sec:flight-exposure} identifies distinct pre-ignition pressure and flow-energy peaks and a later normal-load peak during the burn. The improved touchdown results do not remove these load requirements. A design with lower allowables would need a different descent trajectory and a revised propellant budget; the present contract does not provide those allowables.
\subsection{Ship Placement, Wind, and Deck Motion}
Downrange placement can avoid a return maneuver. Once the ship is positioned, the guidance must target its location and meet the speed and attitude limits relative to its deck. The present stationary, horizontal deck sets the reference for the fixed-site examples. A moving deck would alter the target position, relative velocity, and contact orientation.

\section{Reproducibility and Source Code}
From the repository root, run \texttt{node src/run-planar.mjs} to reproduce the demonstration trajectories and \texttt{node src/verify-p1.mjs} to reproduce the P1 numerical comparisons.

Run \texttt{node src/p3-scenarios.mjs} to reproduce the fixed-ship and atmosphere coverage cases. The command reads the reference contract and the selected P2 command history, then writes \path{results/p3-scenarios.json}.
The following commands compute the reference corrective schedule and rebuild the full-flight comparison from the saved schedules.
\begin{verbatim}
python src/p3-replan.py --case nominal
python src/p3-replan.py --replay-all
\end{verbatim}
The schedules are stored in \path{results/p3/replanned/}. The planner uses the dependencies in \path{requirements-p2.txt}.
Run \texttt{python src/p3-tracking.py} to prepare the control-margin reference and regenerate the tracking gains and two-step-size comparison in \path{results/p3/tracking/}.
Run \texttt{node src/p3-exposure.mjs} followed by \texttt{python src/plot-p3-exposure.py} to generate the full-flight environment records in \path{results/p3/safety/} and their figure. The incoming flow-energy indicator is not a wall-heating calculation.
Run \texttt{python src/p3-propulsion.py --isp 330}, followed by the same command with \texttt{--isp 300} and \texttt{--isp 360}, to reproduce the effective-efficiency scenarios. Run \texttt{python src/plot-p3-propulsion.py} to generate their budget figure. The saved candidates and scenario budgets are in \path{results/p3/propulsion/}.
