\section{Assumptions}
The booster is initially approximated as a rigid circular cylinder. This external geometry does not establish a uniform internal mass distribution. Atmospheric drag is retained using air-relative velocity and altitude-dependent density. The baseline translational model assumes planar motion and zero wind; steady uniform wind is a subsequent scenario, rather than a justification for neglecting aerodynamic torque.

Aerodynamic moments depend on the offset between the center of pressure and center of mass. A prescribed-axis translational model therefore cannot establish attitude stability or vertical touchdown. Rotational dynamics and control authority must be assessed separately.

Earth rotation is provisionally omitted from the baseline and requires a quantitative sensitivity check. Its omission is not justified by freedom in ship placement: Coriolis acceleration can alter velocity and consequently aerodynamic loading. The initial flight-path angle and all unspecified engine and aerodynamic parameters remain explicit scenario inputs.

The deck is initially stationary and horizontal. Position denotes the booster center of mass; contact geometry must account for vehicle length and, when specified, landing gear and deck height.

\subsection{Reference Parameter Selection}
Guided by the cited propulsion and aerodynamic references, we define a reference design rather than identify an unspecified flight engine. We adopt a maximum landing thrust of 2.846 MN using the published BE-4 thrust scale~\cite{be4}. We assume an effective specific impulse of 330 s, oxygen-to-methane mass ratio of 3.5, minimum operating throttle of 40\%, and gimbal limit of $10^\circ$. Except for the analogous thrust scale, these are explicit design assumptions, not manufacturer specifications for this booster. The mixture-ratio choice is informed by a methane/oxygen mission study~\cite{methaneStudy}; its vacuum performance is not assigned to our sea-level engine.

For a tractable first model, axial and crossflow coefficients are set to 0.8 and 1.2, respectively. Cylinder research motivates separating the flow components~\cite{cylinder}, but these constants are engineering closures rather than fitted experimental data. The equivalent center-of-pressure offset is assumed to be $+0.05L$ toward the nose. Standard-atmosphere properties follow the 1976 model~\cite{atmosphere}. A trial inventory of 80 tonnes and initial flight-path angle of $15^\circ$ define the full-trajectory demonstration; this inventory is not an optimized fuel estimate.

\section{Model Motion Analysis}
\subsection{Axial and Crossflow Aerodynamic Model}
Let $\mathbf e=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $\mathbf n=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$, with $\theta$ measured counterclockwise from horizontal. Resolve air-relative velocity into $u_a$ and $u_n$. For equal-length equivalent strips with signed moment arms $s_i$,
\begin{align}
 F_a&=-\tfrac12\rho C_a A_{end}u_a|u_a|,\\
 F_{n,i}&=-\tfrac12\rho C_n D\Delta s\,(u_n+\omega s_i)|u_n+\omega s_i|,\\
 \mathbf F_D&=F_a\mathbf e+\sum_i F_{n,i}\mathbf n,\qquad M_a=\sum_i s_iF_{n,i}.
\end{align}
The strip distribution incorporates rotational damping and an assumed pressure-center offset. It is a low-order closure, not a resolved surface-pressure calculation.
\subsection{Equations of Translational Motion}
\begin{align}
 \dot{\mathbf r}&=\mathbf v, &
 \dot{\mathbf v}&=\mathbf g(h)+\frac{\mathbf F_D+T\mathbf e(\theta+\delta)}{m},\\
 \dot m&=-\frac{T}{g_0I_{sp}}, &
 \dot\theta&=\omega,\qquad
 I_d\dot\omega=M_a-\frac{L}{2}T\sin\delta.
\end{align}
Here $I_d=m_d(3R^2+L^2)/12$. The dry body is uniform and the remaining propellant is idealized as concentrated at the center of mass with negligible pitch inertia and no additional rotational angular-momentum flux. This explicit simplification keeps $I_d$ constant; a distributed-tank model requires a consistent open-system angular-momentum balance.
