\renewcommand{\abstractname}{Summary}
\begin{abstract}
We analyze booster descent, vertical touchdown, and separation propellant using coupled translation, pitch, and mass-loss equations. Using the stated dry mass and reference propulsion and aerodynamic assumptions, a $15^\circ$ separation trajectory coasts for 176.68 s and then uses a 30.79 s landing burn, with the ship placed 278.31 km downrange. The accepted local candidate carries approximately 26.23 t of combined propellant: 5.83 t methane and 20.40 t liquid oxygen. About 21.15 t is consumed and 5.08 t remains, including the selected 5 t usable reserve. Position, speed, tilt, and pitch rate meet the adopted touchdown conditions in full-flight replay. Known-efficiency cases at 300--360 s specific impulse give inventories of 24.20--28.78 t; a lower-crossflow case remains unresolved. Initial-direction cases also demonstrate that the speed magnitude alone does not determine fuel demand. The nominal trajectory reaches approximately 297 kPa dynamic pressure and $7.70\,g_0$ specific force. Control-slew demands and a 278 km flight expose additional actuator and planar-geometry limitations. Full-flight structural and thermal safety is not established because load allowables and local thermal response are undetermined. The reported inventory is a conditional model result, not a universal or globally minimal fuel requirement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background}
Reusable first-stage boosters require controlled descent and a safe vertical landing before refurbishment and reuse.
\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}
At separation, the booster has an altitude of 70 km and a speed of 6000 km/h. The stated dry mass is 130000 kg, its height is 65 m, and its diameter is 7.0 m. It uses liquid oxygen and methane. The recovery ship may be placed at a convenient location. The task is to analyze a safe descent and controlled vertical landing, and estimate the fuel required at separation~\cite{problem}. The total separation mass is the stated dry mass plus the remaining combined propellant.
The problem specifies the initial speed magnitude. The direction affects the time aloft, downrange distance, and state at landing-burn ignition, so it is an explicit scenario input. We use \emph{propellant} for the combined mass of liquid oxygen and methane and \emph{fuel} for the methane component.
\section{Assumptions}
The booster is initially approximated as a rigid circular cylinder. This external geometry does not establish a uniform internal mass distribution. Atmospheric drag is retained using air-relative velocity and altitude-dependent density. The baseline translational model assumes planar motion and zero wind; steady uniform wind is a subsequent scenario, rather than a justification for neglecting aerodynamic torque.

Aerodynamic moments depend on the offset between the center of pressure and center of mass. A prescribed-axis translational model therefore cannot establish attitude stability or vertical touchdown. Rotational dynamics and control authority must be assessed separately.

Earth rotation is provisionally omitted from the baseline and requires a quantitative sensitivity check. Its omission is not justified by freedom in ship placement: Coriolis acceleration can alter velocity and consequently aerodynamic loading. The initial flight-path angle and all unspecified engine and aerodynamic parameters remain explicit scenario inputs.

The deck is initially stationary and horizontal. Position denotes the booster center of mass; contact geometry must account for vehicle length and, when specified, landing gear and deck height.

\subsection{Strengths and Weaknesses}
The model accounts for changing mass and pitch motion while locating the first contact of the tilted body with the deck. This makes the landing state and propellant budget mutually consistent. Its principal limitations are assumed propulsion and aerodynamic parameters, ideal actuator response, simplified inertia, and planar motion. Numerical agreement within this model does not establish the accuracy of those physical assumptions. The parameter comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:p3} address some of their effect on the answer, while leaving structural and thermal safety undetermined.
\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\subsection{Attitude Stability and Vertical Touchdown}
The reference trajectory satisfies position, relative speed, tilt, and pitch-rate conditions at the same contact event. Upright attitude alone would not establish a controlled landing. During engine-off coast the model has no active attitude actuator; during the burn, correction is limited by thrust and gimbal authority. These restrictions qualify the nominal touchdown result.
\input{paper/p4-design-checks.tex}
\subsection{Ship Placement, Wind, and Deck Motion}
Downrange placement can avoid a return maneuver. Once the ship is positioned, the guidance must target its location and meet the speed and attitude limits relative to its deck. The present stationary, horizontal deck sets the reference for the fixed-site examples. A moving deck would alter the target position, relative velocity, and contact orientation.
\subsection{Conclusions and Practical Recommendations}
For the selected $15^\circ$ reference case, a coast followed by a single landing burn provides a complete trajectory to a conveniently placed ship and an accepted local separation inventory of approximately 26.23 t. With the assumed oxygen-to-methane ratio of 3.5, this is about 5.83 t methane and 20.40 t oxygen, including the chosen 5 t retained-reserve requirement. This conditional budget uses the stated dry mass and the adopted model assumptions. The 278 km downrange solution also requires a spherical-Earth extension before meter-scale physical interception can be claimed. The unresolved aerodynamic search is excluded from feasible fuel ranges. Full-flight structural and thermal safety has not been established; the calculated load peaks identify environmental requirements, not proof that the booster survives them. Consequently, a final inventory satisfying demonstrated structural and thermal limits remains undetermined.
