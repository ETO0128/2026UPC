\upctitle{Controlled Descent and Propellant Estimation\\for a First-Stage Rocket Booster}
\renewcommand{\abstractname}{Summary}
\begin{abstract}
We model a first-stage booster on a nonrotating spherical Earth with coupled translation, pitch, aerodynamics, and propellant consumption. For a 130 t dry mass and a $15^\circ$ climb angle at separation, the lowest verified inventory is 25.97 t, comprising 5.77 t of methane and 20.20 t of liquid oxygen. The booster coasts for 181 s and ignites once at 6.2 km altitude, burning 20.89 t and retaining the required 5 t reserve. It touches down upright after 213.25 s, with the ship at 284.12 km downrange, a downward speed of 1.51 m/s, and a horizontal speed of 0.003 m/s. Independent replay meets the touchdown limits at steps down to 0.00125 s. Among the parameters varied without a pressure cap, the separation direction has the largest effect. Level and $30^\circ$ separations use 24.32 and 32.46 t, respectively. Varying effective specific impulse from 300 to 360 s gives 28.48 to 23.98 t, while the tested crossflow coefficients change the inventory by less than 0.5 t. The nominal descent reaches 293 kPa dynamic pressure and an estimated 130--250 kW/m$^2$ stagnation heat flux before ignition. With an extra braking burn, the lowest verified inventories for 200 and 150 kPa caps are \QcapTwoHundred{} and \QcapOneFifty{} t. These capped values are feasible upper estimates. All results are conditional on the reference rigid-body, stationary-deck, and ideal-actuator assumptions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background}
A reusable first stage has to come back intact. After separation it is still moving at several times the speed of sound, high in the atmosphere, and it must lose that speed, turn upright, and settle onto a landing site gently enough to be flown again. For a sea landing the recovery ship can be placed under the descent path, so the booster does not have to fly back toward the launch site. What remains is to brake, to keep the attitude under control, and to touch down slowly with some propellant in reserve. The propellant carried for this descent is mass that the first stage cannot use during ascent, so the size of the descent budget matters for the whole vehicle.

This paper estimates that budget. Sections~\ref{sec:assumptions}--\ref{sec:motion} set out the assumptions and the force and motion model, including the correction for a spherical Earth. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} finds a minimum-propellant landing strategy, reports the separation inventory, and shows how it depends on the separation direction. Section~\ref{sec:p3} examines engine efficiency, aerodynamic drag, and the loads along the descent, and the final sections evaluate the model and state the conclusions.
\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}
At separation the booster is at 70 km altitude and moves at 6000 km/h. It is 65 m tall and 7.0 m in diameter, and the problem figure gives its dry mass as 130000 kg. It burns liquid oxygen and methane. The recovery ship may be placed wherever is convenient. We are asked to describe a safe descent and a controlled vertical landing and to estimate how much fuel the booster must carry at separation~\cite{problem}. Its total mass at separation is therefore the dry mass plus the propellant on board.

The problem gives the speed at separation but not its direction. The direction sets how long the booster stays aloft, how far it travels downrange, and its state when the landing burn begins. It is fixed by the ascent mission, not by the recovery plan, so we treat it as an input, with $15^\circ$ above horizontal as the reference (Section~\ref{sec:separation-direction}). Throughout, \emph{propellant} means the combined mass of liquid oxygen and methane, \emph{fuel} means the methane alone, and the \emph{inventory} is the propellant mass on board at separation.

\subsection{Strengths and Weaknesses}
The main strength of the model is that the trajectory, the attitude, and the propellant budget come from one calculation. The landing state and the fuel figure therefore describe the same flight. The model includes changing mass, pitch dynamics with aerodynamic damping, Earth curvature, and contact between the tilted cylinder and the curved sea surface, and every reported solution is checked by an independent replay at two time steps.

Its weaknesses are the assumed engine and aerodynamic parameters, which are not fitted to a real vehicle; ideal actuators without rate limits; a simplified mass distribution; a rigid structure with no structural assessment and only an order-of-magnitude heating estimate; the neglect of Earth rotation and out-of-plane motion; and solutions that are local optima. Agreement within the model says nothing about the accuracy of these assumptions. Section~\ref{sec:p3} shows how much some of them move the answer.
\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\subsection{Attitude Stability and Vertical Touchdown}
The reference trajectory meets the position, speed, tilt, and pitch-rate limits at the same instant of contact. All four are needed: an upright booster that is still drifting or rotating has not landed under control. While the engine is off, nothing in the model controls the attitude; during the burn, the available correction is limited by thrust and gimbal travel. The nominal touchdown result holds under these limits.

\subsection{Ship Placement, Wind, and Deck Motion}
Placing the ship downrange, under the descent path, removes the need to fly back and allows a single landing burn. Once the ship is in place, however, the booster has to reach that point, so any wind or other error not known in advance must be corrected in flight. The ship here is stationary with a level deck. A moving deck would change the target position, the relative velocity at contact, and the deck orientation.
\subsection{Conclusions and Practical Recommendations}
For the spherical-Earth reference with a $15^\circ$ separation direction, a coast followed by one landing burn needs 25.97 t of propellant at separation, the lowest value we found. At an oxygen-to-methane mass ratio of 3.5 this is 5.77 t of methane and 20.20 t of liquid oxygen, and it includes the 5 t usable reserve. The ship is placed 284.12 km downrange along the surface, and the trajectory meets all contact limits. The flat-Earth version of the same design needed 26.23 t, with the ship at 278.31 km.

The answer depends most on inputs that the problem leaves open. The separation direction, which the ascent mission fixes, moves the requirement from 24.32 t at $0^\circ$ to 32.46 t at $30^\circ$, and the ship moves from 199 to 349 km downrange. Effective specific impulse between 300 and 360 s gives 28.48 to 23.98 t, whereas crossflow drag between $C_n=0.8$ and 1.6 changes the result by less than 0.5 t. In practice, the separation direction and the engine efficiency should be fixed first, because together they determine both the propellant load and where the ship must wait.

The reference descent reaches 293 kPa dynamic pressure before ignition. With a separate entry burn, we found feasible inventories of about \QcapTwoHundred{} t at a 200 kPa cap and \QcapOneFifty{} t at a 150 kPa cap (Section~\ref{sec:qcap}). These are upper estimates within the tested strategy family. All figures assume the reference engine and drag parameters, a nonrotating Earth, a stationary ship, ideal actuators, and a rigid structure.
