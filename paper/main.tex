\renewcommand{\abstractname}{Summary}
\begin{abstract}
We model the controlled descent and vertical landing of a first-stage booster using coupled translation, pitch, aerodynamic forces, and propellant consumption. Earth curvature is retained through radial altitude, surface arc distance, curvature acceleration, and the changing local horizontal. For the stated 130 t dry mass and a selected $15^\circ$ separation direction, the reoptimized spherical-Earth trajectory requires 26.04 t of liquid oxygen and methane at separation. This comprises 5.79 t methane and 20.25 t oxygen, including a selected 5 t usable reserve. Approximately 20.96 t is consumed and 5.08 t remains. A conveniently positioned ship lies 284.16 km downrange in surface arc distance. Contact occurs at 213.02 s with horizontal and downward speeds of approximately 0.005 and 1.515 m/s, and local pitch-rate magnitude 0.027$^\circ$/s. Three replay steps down to 0.00125 s satisfy the adopted position, velocity, attitude, angular-rate, and reserve criteria. Vacuum invariants and symbolic-to-numerical derivative comparisons verify the spherical formulation. The preceding planar design gives 26.23 t and a ship distance of 278.31 km. Planar parameter studies show dependence on engine efficiency, initial direction, and crossflow drag, with one low-crossflow search unresolved. The analysis assumes a rigid booster, a nonrotating Earth, a stationary ship, effective propulsion parameters, and ideal actuators. The inventory is an accepted local design under these assumptions.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background}
Reusable first-stage boosters require controlled descent and a safe vertical landing before refurbishment and reuse.
\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}
At separation, the booster has an altitude of 70 km and a speed of 6000 km/h. The stated dry mass is 130000 kg, its height is 65 m, and its diameter is 7.0 m. It uses liquid oxygen and methane. The recovery ship may be placed at a convenient location. The task is to analyze a safe descent and controlled vertical landing, and estimate the fuel required at separation~\cite{problem}. The total separation mass is the stated dry mass plus the remaining combined propellant.
The problem specifies the initial speed magnitude. The direction affects the time aloft, downrange distance, and state at landing-burn ignition, so it is an explicit scenario input. We use \emph{propellant} for the combined mass of liquid oxygen and methane and \emph{fuel} for the methane component.
\section{Assumptions}
The booster is initially approximated as a rigid circular cylinder. This external geometry does not establish a uniform internal mass distribution. Atmospheric drag is retained using air-relative velocity and altitude-dependent density. The baseline translational model assumes planar motion and zero wind; steady uniform wind is a subsequent scenario, rather than a justification for neglecting aerodynamic torque.

Aerodynamic moments depend on the offset between the center of pressure and center of mass. A prescribed-axis translational model therefore cannot establish attitude stability or vertical touchdown. Rotational dynamics and control authority must be assessed separately.

The corrected reference uses a nonrotating spherical Earth. Radial altitude and the moving local horizontal enter the dynamics, atmosphere queries, and contact geometry. Initial direction and the unspecified propulsion and aerodynamic parameters are explicit scenario inputs. The booster is rigid, with structural capacity and material failure excluded from the model scope.

The deck is initially stationary and horizontal. Position denotes the booster center of mass; contact geometry must account for vehicle length and, when specified, landing gear and deck height.

\subsection{Strengths and Weaknesses}
The model accounts for changing mass and pitch motion while locating the first contact of the tilted body with the deck. This makes the landing state and propellant budget mutually consistent. Its principal limitations are assumed propulsion and aerodynamic parameters, ideal actuator response, simplified inertia, and omission of Earth rotation. Numerical agreement within this model does not establish the accuracy of those physical assumptions. The parameter comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:p3} address some of their effect on the answer, within the rigid-body modeling scope.
\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\subsection{Attitude Stability and Vertical Touchdown}
The reference trajectory satisfies position, relative speed, tilt, and pitch-rate conditions at the same contact event. Upright attitude alone would not establish a controlled landing. During engine-off coast the model has no active attitude actuator; during the burn, correction is limited by thrust and gimbal authority. These restrictions qualify the nominal touchdown result.
\input{paper/p4-design-checks.tex}
\subsection{Ship Placement, Wind, and Deck Motion}
Downrange placement can avoid a return maneuver. Once the ship is positioned, the guidance must target its location and meet the speed and attitude limits relative to its deck. The present stationary, horizontal deck sets the reference for the fixed-site examples. A moving deck would alter the target position, relative velocity, and contact orientation.
\subsection{Conclusions and Practical Recommendations}
For the spherical-Earth reference with a $15^\circ$ separation direction, a coast followed by one landing burn gives an accepted local separation inventory of 26.038 t. At the selected oxygen-to-methane mass ratio of 3.5, this is 5.786 t methane and 20.252 t oxygen. The 5 t usable-reserve requirement is included. The recovery ship is placed at 284.160 km surface arc distance, and the full rigid-body trajectory satisfies the adopted contact constraints.

The corresponding planar design used 26.228 t and a ship distance of 278.310 km. Reoptimization with consistent moving-frame dynamics corrects both the trajectory and its target location. Engine-efficiency, separation-direction, and aerodynamic comparisons from the planar baseline quantify assumption dependence, while a lower-crossflow case remains unresolved. These separate studies do not define a universal inventory for all parameter combinations. The reported solution assumes the reference propulsion and aerodynamics, a nonrotating Earth, a stationary ship, and ideal actuators. Structural capacity is outside the rigid-body study.\clearpage
